What the Impact Player rule changed

Since 2023 an IPL side can bring in a twelfth player mid-match. Most of the argument about it is over 200-run totals. I went through ball-by-ball data from 2,017 full-length matches, and the biggest change I found is in who bowls.

1. Top-seven batters bowl half as much

From 2019 to 2022, an IPL side's top seven batters bowled about 25% of its deliveries. In 2026 it was 12.6%. Most of that comes from who teams pick for the top seven (section 4).

SMAT is the useful case here. Same competition, mostly the same players, and the rule went in and then came out, and the bowling share moved with it both times. Against the Blast and CPL, with season fixed effects, the rule cut the share by 7.8 points in the IPL and 9.9 in SMAT (95% range −12.7 to −7.1). Once SMAT dropped it, the gap shrank to −2.4 points, which is within noise.

2. Scoring went up, and the rule explains part of it

IPL first-innings totals in full 20-over matches went from 160.7 a game (2019–22) to 183.3 (2023–26). That's 23 runs, and I don't think the rule earns all of it.

SMAT points the same way. With the rule, its first-innings average was 140 and then 144, against 142 to 147 before. In the two seasons after the rule was dropped it jumped to 163. Indian batters started going harder around 2024 whether or not there was a twelfth player. Measured against leagues that never had the rule, the part of the IPL rise the rule can be credited with is about 10 to 15 runs, and one of my two comparisons can't rule out zero.

3. The extra batter didn't make wickets cheaper

The usual case for the rule is that an extra player deepens the batting, so the top order can attack without worrying about a collapse. If that's how it works, an early wicket should cost less than it used to. I couldn't find that. With the score at 10 overs held fixed, each wicket down at that point cost an IPL side 6.3 runs off its final total before the rule and 7.2 after. The ranges overlap, and if anything it moved the wrong way. Wickets lost per innings stayed at about 6.2.

So teams mostly use the twelfth player to bring in a bowler. The faster scoring looks like a choice batters made, and SMAT batters kept making it after the rule was gone.

4. Who it affected

Halving the top-seven share doesn't mean every all-rounder bowls half as much. Teams could be picking fewer bowling options in the top seven, or the same players could be bowling less, and the data can pull the two apart. Before the rule a side had 1.84 top-seven batters who bowled in a match. After it, 1.34. Those who did bowl sent down 2.69 overs before and 2.47 after. Roughly four-fifths of the drop is selection.

The regulars haven't changed much. Jadeja, Axar Patel, Narine, Krunal Pandya and Sam Curran are each within about half an over a game of where they were. Hardik Pandya bowls more, but he bowled nothing in 2020 and 2021 after his back injury, so his earlier number starts low.

I'd be careful pinning the big drops on the rule. Rahul Tewatia stopped bowling in 2022, after his role changed at Gujarat, a season before the rule existed. Washington Sundar's fall is mostly 2025 and 2026, as he moved up the order (0.6 overs a game in 2026). Andre Russell's fell to 1.1 overs a game in 2023, the first rule season, though age may be part of that.

What the rule took away is the part-time sixth bowler in the top seven. The player picked for two overs and some runs is the one losing out. Teams now play a specialist batter in that slot and bring in a specialist bowler when they need one.

5. The top three got faster, not more valuable

Totals say how many runs were scored. They don't say whether the runs were worth more. For that I used four ball-by-ball measures from Himanish Ganjoo's T20 primer. Each one compares what a batter did with what an average batter does in the same match state (balls left, wickets down, and the par score or target):

  • RAA, runs above average: scoring speed only.
  • WAA, wickets above average: dismissals avoided.
  • Impact: how far the ball moved the side's projected total, so a wicket costs runs.
  • WPA, win probability added: how far the ball moved the side's chance of winning.

I split batters by order and compared the change in the IPL with the change in the Blast and CPL over the same seasons.

The IPL's extra scoring speed, beyond what other leagues gained, sits in the top three: 6.9 runs per 100 balls faster than the average batter in the same situation. Batters four to seven gained no more than the Blast and CPL did.

That speed didn't turn into more runs added. The top three's Impact barely moved, because they also got out more often (WAA −0.35 per 100 balls in the IPL), and together they now face about 60 balls an innings, against 65 before the rule. They scored faster and paid for it in wickets, which fits section 3. Nor did win probability move toward the top order. Both sides have a twelfth player, so the league as a whole can't win more. What could have changed is which batters win games, and that didn't.

The rule era's most valuable batters aren't all hitters. Kohli and Gill score at close to the average rate for their situations (RAA about +1 to +2 per 100 balls) and are still in the top ten, because they get out less than an average batter would. Iyer heads the list with good but not extreme speed, and he doesn't give his wicket away. Sooryavanshi is the exception, worth two wins in 401 balls on speed alone.

What it means

At an auction I'd still pay for a real all-rounder's bowling, because it still gets used. I wouldn't pay much for a batter's part-time overs. Under this rule the sixth-bowler option rarely gets the ball.

For India's selectors, the worry that the IPL gives batting all-rounders fewer overs holds up, mostly because fewer of them are picked to bat in the top seven. SMAT went back to eleven players and now gives its top-seven batters about 2.4 times the IPL's share of the bowling.

And on 200-run totals, blaming the rule overstates it. Domestic batters got faster in seasons when the rule was gone. Where the IPL did pull ahead, in its top three, the faster scoring came with more wickets, and it didn't make those batters more valuable to their sides.

Method and limits

  • Ball-by-ball commentary from bcci.tv (IPL, SMAT) and Cricket Australia's match centre (T20 Blast, CPL), 2019 to 2026. I kept only matches where both innings ran the full 20 overs, so rain-cut games don't drag totals down.
  • The comparison is a difference in differences with season fixed effects and ground (for totals) or league (for bowling share) fixed effects, robust standard errors. Ground effects absorb the IPL's 2020–21 seasons in the UAE and 2022 in Mumbai and Pune. BBL is left out because its X-Factor substitute (2020-21 to 2022-23) is a similar rule.
  • RAA, WAA, Impact and WPA follow Himanish Ganjoo's T20 Metrics primer: nested-shrinkage par scores, a Duckworth-Lewis Pro run curve, an xgboost baseline on balls left, wickets down, par or target and innings, and win probability P = rn/(1 + rn) from the DL score ratio. My implementation is fit to this data, not centred per season, and agrees with the primer's published IPL 2026 tables to about one run of RAA per 100 balls; its WPA correlates with them at 0.997. Wides are left out of player numbers, as in the primer. A non-striker run out counts against the batter who was out.
  • Batting order is taken as the order in which batters faced their first ball. Bowling share uses only sides that used at least seven batters, so positions one to seven are all known.
  • SMAT coverage is partial after 2021: only matches with commentary on bcci.tv, 34 full-length matches in 2023. SMAT estimates are less precise than the IPL ones.
  • The two-bouncer-per-over rule came to SMAT in 2023 and the IPL in 2024. SMAT kept it after dropping the Impact Player, and the bowling share still recovered, so the bouncer rule doesn't explain the bowling result. It may add to the scoring pattern.
  • Rule dates for SMAT come from news reports of the BCCI decision in October 2024, for example ThePrint and Deccan Herald. I've assumed 2025-26 was played without it.

Every number here is printed by impact_player.py in the project code.
